Best Power Station for a 3-Day Power Outage (Complete Guide)

Category: Buying Guides Intent: Commercial ~8 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

A 3-day outage is a different sizing problem than an overnight one — not just bigger, but structurally different, because you almost certainly can't rely on a single charge lasting the whole time. This guide works through the full framework: what to run, how to budget across multiple days, and when a portable power station stops being the right tool on its own.

Why "just buy a bigger battery" doesn't fully solve this

It's tempting to take your daily energy need and multiply by three. In practice, that pushes capacity requirements into a range — often 6,000-10,000Wh or more for a household running a fridge — where the unit itself becomes very large, very heavy, and very expensive, and you're still assuming zero recharging for 72 hours straight. A more realistic plan treats capacity and recharging as two separate levers, not one.

Step 1: Build your daily "must-run" budget

Use the same tiered approach from our sizing guide: Tier 1 (medical equipment, a fridge, communication), Tier 2 (a few lights, a fan, device charging), Tier 3 (skip it — heating elements, HVAC, multiple major appliances). For a 3-day plan, be more disciplined about Tier 3 than you might be for a single night, since every watt you don't need to run is a watt you don't need to recharge later.

LoadRunning wattsHours/dayDaily Wh
Refrigerator (cycling)150W8h effective1,200
Wi-Fi router10W16h160

Phone/laptop charging65W3h195
Daily raw total~1,755 Wh/day

Adjusted for usable capacity (÷0.85, per Watt-Hours Explained): roughly 2,065Wh per day. Over 3 days with zero recharging, that's over 6,000Wh — which is why the recharging plan matters as much as the battery size.

Step 2: Decide your recharging strategy before you decide capacity

There are three realistic paths, and they lead to very different equipment decisions:

  • No recharging planned (worst case, grid and everything else down): you need the full multi-day capacity upfront — often two units or one large expandable-capacity unit, sized to the full 3-day total with margin.
  • Solar recharging: realistically recovers a fraction of daily use per day of decent sun — see how many solar panels you actually need before assuming this closes the gap. Solar works better as a way to stretch a smaller unit than as a full daily recharge.
  • Vehicle or generator-assisted recharging: if you can safely run a car (outdoors, never in an attached garage) or a small generator for an hour or two daily, you can recharge a meaningfully smaller unit repeatedly rather than owning capacity for the whole outage upfront.

For most households planning around genuine multi-day risk (hurricane-prone or wildfire-PSPS regions especially), two mid-size units with a rotation plan is often the more resilient choice, even if it's marginally less capacity-efficient on paper.

Step 4: What to prioritize cutting if you fall short

  1. Cut discretionary electronics (TV, gaming, non-essential charging) first — lowest cost to lifestyle, meaningful energy savings.
  2. Reduce fridge door openings and consider consolidating food into a cooler with ice if the outage stretches past day 2, freeing that budget entirely.
  3. Charge devices during daylight if solar is available, rather than running lights and charging simultaneously after dark.
  4. Never cut medical equipment power to extend other loads — see our medical backup planning guide for how to build margin in specifically for that category instead.

When to stop trying to solve this with portable units alone

If your household's Tier 1 list alone exceeds what two mid-size portable units can realistically deliver over 3 days — multiple medical devices, a well pump, or an expectation of longer outages — see Portable Power Station vs Gas Generator and consider a professionally installed whole-home backup system as the primary plan, with portable units as the mobile/backup layer rather than the main one.

How the recharging picture differs by outage cause

"3 days" isn't one scenario — the realistic recharging opportunity varies a lot by what's causing the outage, and that should shape which strategy from Step 2 you lean on:

  • Hurricanes and tropical storms: often several days of advance warning, but frequently overcast or rainy conditions during and after — solar recharging is typically weaker than you'd hope right when you need it most. Lean more heavily on pre-charging everything fully before landfall and on vehicle/generator recharging if safely available afterward.
  • Winter storms and grid failures: cold reduces usable battery capacity (see Watt-Hours Explained) and short winter daylight hours limit solar input even on clear days. Size with extra margin specifically for cold-weather capacity loss.
  • Wildfire-related public safety power shutoffs (PSPS): often clear, sunny conditions (part of why the shutoff was called in the first place), which makes solar recharging genuinely more viable than in the other two scenarios — but PSPS events can also extend unpredictably past the initially announced window, so build in schedule margin too.
  • Grid equipment failures unrelated to weather: the least predictable category — no advance warning to pre-charge around, which makes maintaining your equipment at or near full charge as routine practice (see our maintenance checklist) more valuable than for weather-driven outages you can see coming.

A concrete pre-outage checklist

Once you know your recharging strategy and capacity plan, here's what to actually have ready before day 1 of the outage, not during it:

  1. Every power station charged to 100%, tested within the past month to confirm it actually holds charge (batteries degrade, and the first time to find that out shouldn't be during an outage).
  2. Charging cables for every device, plus a spare for your power station's own charging cable specifically — this is an easy thing to lose track of and hard to improvise.
  3. A written, prioritized list of what gets powered first if capacity runs tighter than planned (see Step 4 above) — deciding this calmly in advance beats deciding it during an actual shortage.
  4. If using solar as part of your plan, panels tested and cables/connectors checked for wear before you need them.
  5. A non-electric backup for your single most critical need, where possible — a manual can opener, a battery (not USB-dependent) flashlight, a wind-up or battery radio for updates if cell service is also affected.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to buy one very large power station or two smaller ones for a 3-day outage?

For most households, two mid-size units offer better redundancy and recharging flexibility than one large unit, even at a small efficiency cost — see the comparison above.

Can solar panels realistically keep a power station running for 3 days?

Solar can meaningfully stretch a unit's runtime, but rarely fully replaces daily usage on its own — see our guide on realistic solar charging expectations before relying on it as your only recharging plan.

How much capacity do I actually need for 3 days?

It depends entirely on your must-run list and recharging plan — a household running only lights and device charging might need under 1,500Wh total with a recharging plan, while one running a fridge with zero recharging could need over 6,000Wh. Best Power Station for CPAP Machines During a Power Outage

Category: Buying Guides Intent: Commercial ~6 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

For a CPAP machine, "good enough" isn't really the standard — this is equipment tied to sleep and, for many users, to broader health management. This guide focuses on how to evaluate options against that higher bar, rather than pushing a single "best" pick without knowing your specific machine and situation.

Talk to your DME supplier first Your durable medical equipment (DME) provider or sleep clinic can confirm your exact machine's power draw and may have specific guidance or approved backup options. Treat this guide as a framework for evaluating a power station, not a substitute for that conversation — especially if you use supplemental oxygen alongside your CPAP, which changes the equipment and safety picture.

What to check before comparing any specific models

  1. Your machine's actual wattage — from its label or manual, including with a heated humidifier if you use one. See watt-hours explained for how to read this correctly.
  2. Pure sine wave output — CPAP machines are electronics with internal power supplies that can run inefficiently, run hot, or occasionally show errors on modified sine wave power. A pure sine wave inverter (standard on most mid-range and higher power stations today, but worth confirming) is the safer default.
  3. Coverage for at least 2 full nights — one night of margin isn't really margin. If your calculated need is ~400Wh for one night, look at units in the 700–1,000Wh range or larger so a delayed outage or a bad recharging day doesn't leave you short.
  4. A genuinely quiet fan/no fan under light load — some units run an active cooling fan even at low draw, which matters if the unit will sit in a bedroom overnight.
  5. An always-on / no-auto-shutoff mode — some power stations auto-shut-off at very low current draw to save power, which is a serious problem for a device meant to run all night at low, steady wattage. This is worth confirming directly in the product manual or with the manufacturer, not assuming.

Don't rely on a single point of failure

For health-critical equipment, most guidance from patient advocacy and DME resources points toward layered backup rather than a single battery: know your local options for medical priority status with your utility, keep a plan for where to go if backup power isn't enough, and treat a portable power station as one part of a plan — not the whole plan.

Budget vs Mid-Range vs Premium Power Stations: What You Actually Get at Each Price Tier

Category: Buying Guides Intent: Comparative ~7 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

Portable power station prices span roughly $150 to $5,000+ for broadly similar-looking boxes. Here's what actually tends to differ between price tiers — and where spending more buys you something real versus just a bigger number on the spec sheet.

Budget tier (roughly under $300)

At this tier, you're most likely to encounter:

  • Lithium-ion (NMC) rather than LiFePO4 batteries — see our battery chemistry comparison; this generally means shorter cycle life, though lower weight.
  • Modified sine wave inverters more often — check this specifically; see why it matters before assuming compatibility with sensitive electronics or medical equipment.
  • Lower continuous wattage ratings — often 200-500W, which rules out larger appliances even briefly.
  • Fewer ports and no app connectivity — usually just AC outlets, USB-A, and a car port.
  • Shorter warranties — commonly 1 year versus 2-5 years at higher tiers.

This tier is genuinely fine for light device charging, short camping trips, and non-critical backup — it becomes a false economy when the use case involves medical equipment, a refrigerator, or anything safety-critical.

Mid-range tier (roughly $300-$800)

This is where LiFePO4 batteries and pure sine wave inverters become standard rather than exceptional, continuous wattage typically climbs to 500-1,500W (enough for a refrigerator and modest simultaneous loads), and app connectivity/monitoring starts appearing. Warranties commonly extend to 2-3 years. For most households sizing around home backup, camping, or RV use as described elsewhere on this site, this tier is where the sizing math from Watt-Hours Explained tends to land.

Premium tier (roughly $800-$2,000+)

Beyond mid-range pricing, you're typically paying for:

  • Higher continuous wattage (2,000-3,000W+), enabling larger appliances and multiple simultaneous loads.
  • Expandable battery capacity — additional battery modules that connect to a base unit, letting you scale capacity over time rather than buying your maximum need upfront.
  • Faster AC charging — often full charge in 1-2 hours versus 3-6+ at lower tiers.
  • Higher solar input limits — relevant if you're pairing with panels for extended off-grid use.
  • Longer warranties and higher rated cycle life — often approaching or exceeding 3,000-4,000 cycles.

Whole-home/expandable systems (roughly $2,000-$5,000+)

At the top end, units blur into small-scale home battery systems — designed to integrate with a subpanel, support much larger continuous loads, and expand toward multi-day, multi-kWh capacity. This is the territory where it's worth directly comparing against a professionally installed system; see Portable Power Station vs Gas Generator for that broader comparison.

Where spending more doesn't help

If your actual need — per the calculation in our sizing guide — is genuinely modest (a router and a few lights for an occasional short outage), a premium-tier unit's extra wattage headroom and expandability are capabilities you'll likely never use. Size to your calculated need first, then choose the tier that meets it with reasonable margin — not the other way around.

Marketing claims worth checking skeptically at every tier

  • "X hours of backup power" on the box. This figure almost always assumes a single, small, constant load (often just enough to power a phone charger) — not a realistic household mix. Recalculate using your own devices with the method in Watt-Hours Explained rather than trusting the headline number.
  • Inflated "before" prices next to a permanent-looking "sale." If a product is discounted every single day you check it, the discount isn't meaningful — see our guide to evaluating deals for how to check this properly.
  • Peak wattage presented as if it were continuous wattage. A unit that can briefly surge to a high number for a few seconds is not the same as one that can sustain that output — check for both figures listed separately.
  • "Charges in under 2 hours" without stating the method. Fast AC charging claims sometimes require a specific high-wattage charger sold separately, or don't reflect solar/car charging speeds at all — read the fine print on what charging method the claim refers to.

Resale and long-term value

Battery-powered equipment doesn't hold resale value the way some other electronics do, since a buyer has no easy way to verify remaining battery health or cycle count on a used unit — expect resale value to drop faster than the physical condition alone would suggest, especially past the 2-3 year mark. This is a modest argument for buying based on genuine current need rather than "future-proofing" with a much larger unit than you currently require, since the premium you pay upfront for unused headroom capacity doesn't come back at resale. A warranty that's transferable to a second owner (not all are) is worth checking if resale is a real consideration for you.

Frequently asked questions

Is a budget power station ever a bad idea even for light use?

Generally no — for phone charging, laptops, and short trips without critical equipment, a budget unit is a reasonable choice. The risk is specifically using one for medical equipment or as a sole home-backup source without checking its inverter type and continuous wattage rating first.

Does a higher price always mean a bigger battery?

No — price differences often reflect inverter quality, continuous wattage rating, charging speed, and warranty length as much as raw capacity. Two units with similar watt-hour ratings can differ significantly in price based on these other factors.

How to Power a CPAP Machine While Camping

Category: Camping & Outdoor Intent: Informational ~5 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

A CPAP machine is one of the more forgiving devices to run off a portable power station — it draws relatively modest power — but it's also one where getting the sizing wrong has an immediate, personal consequence: a machine that shuts off partway through the night.

Before anything else Check your specific CPAP machine's power draw on its label or in the manual — it's usually listed in watts, or as volts × amps you can multiply together. Draw varies notably between models and rises significantly if you use a heated humidifier or heated hose, so don't rely on a generic average for a health-related device. If you're unsure, ask your durable medical equipment (DME) supplier or sleep clinic.

Typical power draw (verify against your machine)

ConfigurationApproximate running watts*
CPAP, no humidifier30–40W
CPAP with humidifier (unheated)35–45W
CPAP with heated humidifier / heated hose60–90W+

*General ranges for context only — confirm your machine's actual rating before relying on this for a night's sleep.

Estimating a night's runtime

Using the formula from our watt-hours guide (Wh needed = watts × hours), an 8-hour night at 40W needs roughly 320Wh of energy — before accounting for the usable-capacity margin, which pushes the real target closer to 380Wh. A heated humidifier setup at 75W for 8 hours needs roughly 600Wh, adjusted to around 700Wh usable. Multi-night camping trips without a recharge option multiply that figure accordingly.

Camping-specific tips

  • Turn off the humidifier if you can tolerate it — it's usually the single biggest power draw in a CPAP setup, and skipping it for a trip can roughly halve your energy needs.
  • Solar recharging is a backup, not a guarantee — cloudy days, tree cover, and short winter daylight all reduce solar input meaningfully; don't plan a multi-night trip assuming solar alone will fully recharge overnight usage.
  • Bring a battery with meaningfully more capacity than your calculated minimum — camping conditions (cold nights, imperfect solar, an extra night) eat into margin fast.
  • Test the full setup at home first — run your actual CPAP off your actual power station for a full night before you're relying on it away from an outlet.

Portable Power Station for Overlanding: What to Look For

Category: Camping & Outdoor Intent: Informational ~6 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

Overlanding puts a power station through conditions regular camping doesn't — dust, vibration, water exposure, and days at a time away from any outlet. Here's what actually matters for that use case specifically.

Ingress protection (IP) ratings, explained

An IP rating (e.g., IP54, IP65) describes resistance to solids (first digit, dust) and liquids (second digit, water), on a 0-6 and 0-9 scale respectively. For overlanding, where a unit may ride in an open truck bed, a rooftop box, or a dusty vehicle interior on rough roads:

RatingWhat it meansSuitability
No rating / IP20 or lowerMinimal to no dust/water protectionInterior use only, away from dust and any moisture
IP54Dust-protected (not dust-tight), splash-resistantReasonable for a covered vehicle interior with some dust exposure
IP65+Dust-tight, resistant to water jetsBetter suited to genuinely exposed mounting or unpredictable weather

Most consumer power stations are not highly rated for ingress protection — check the specific unit's rating rather than assuming ruggedness from marketing photos showing outdoor use.

Charging while driving: DC-DC chargers

A DC-DC charger draws from the vehicle's alternator (via the starter battery or an auxiliary battery) to recharge a power station while driving — genuinely useful for overlanding, where daily driving time can double as recharging time without relying on solar or stopping at outlets. Not all power stations support this input method; check specifically for 12V/car-port charging specs and realistic charging wattage over that connection, which is typically much lower than AC wall charging.

Mounting and security

  • Secure it against vibration and sudden stops — a heavy power station becomes a hazard if it's loose during off-road driving or an abrupt stop. A dedicated mount, strap system, or enclosed storage box matters more here than for stationary camping use.
  • Keep it out of direct sun in hot climates — battery performance and longevity both suffer at sustained high temperatures, and a vehicle interior in direct sun can reach extreme temperatures quickly.
  • Plan for temperature swings — desert overlanding in particular can mean very hot days and cold nights; check your unit's rated operating temperature range against your actual trip conditions.

Weight and space trade-offs

Overlanding rigs are typically weight- and space-constrained in ways a car camping trip isn't. Balance capacity against actual need using the method in Watt-Hours Explained rather than defaulting to the largest available unit — a smaller unit recharged via DC-DC charging and solar during a multi-day trip is often more practical than one large unit taking up significant cargo space and weight capacity.

Dust and desert-specific considerations

Fine desert dust is more penetrating than typical outdoor dust, and it finds its way into ports, vents, and seams that a unit's stated IP rating may not have been tested against in that specific form. Beyond checking the IP rating itself, consider a dedicated dust cover or sealed storage case when the unit isn't actively in use, and inspect ports before charging — dust in a port can cause poor connections or, in worse cases, damage over repeated use. Desert environments also mean significant day-to-night temperature swings; a unit baking in direct sun during the day and dropping near freezing overnight experiences more thermal stress than a moderate-climate camping trip would put it through.

Redundancy planning for remote, multi-day trips

The further you are from resupply or help, the more a single point of failure matters. Consider:

  • A smaller backup power source (even just a large power bank for phones and a satellite communicator) separate from your main power station, so a single failure doesn't eliminate all power access.
  • Testing your DC-DC charging setup before the trip, not on day one of a remote route where a wiring issue is much harder to diagnose and fix.
  • Carrying physical maps and a plan that doesn't depend on powered devices as a genuine fallback, not just a backup battery for the same devices.
  • Knowing your power station's behavior in a fault state — does it fail safe (simply stop working) or is there a risk scenario worth understanding before you're relying on it far from help?

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a high IP-rated power station for overlanding?

It depends on how exposed the unit will be — one stored inside a covered vehicle interior needs less protection than one mounted externally or exposed to weather. Match the rating to your actual storage and mounting plan.

Is DC-DC charging as fast as AC wall charging?

Generally no — DC-DC/car charging is typically slower than AC wall charging, but it has the advantage of happening automatically while driving, which suits overlanding's daily-driving-then-camping pattern well.

How to Tell If a Power Station Deal Is Actually a Deal

Category: Deals & Seasonal Intent: Informational ~4 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

A big red "40% OFF" badge tells you nothing about whether a price is actually good. Here's how to check for yourself, in about two minutes, without needing to track prices for months first.

Ignore the percentage. Calculate cost per watt-hour instead.

The only number that lets you compare two different power stations fairly is price ÷ usable watt-hours. A "50% off" unit with a small battery can easily cost more per Wh than a "no discount" unit with a larger one.

Worked example Unit A: $399 for a 500Wh unit → $0.80 per Wh.
Unit B: $549 for a 1,000Wh unit → $0.55 per Wh — genuinely cheaper per unit of capacity, even at a higher sticker price.

Use rated Wh for this comparison since that's what's printed on every listing consistently; just remember actual usable capacity runs somewhat lower across the board, as covered in Watt-Hours Explained — the gap applies roughly evenly when you're comparing similar units, so it doesn't usually change which option is the better deal.

Check whether the "before" price ever actually applied

Some listings show a permanently inflated "list price" that few customers ever paid, making every day look like a discount. Independent price-history tools (widely available as free browser extensions for major retailers) show a product's actual price over the past several months — if the "sale" price matches what it's sold for most of the year, it isn't really a deal.

How Does a Portable Power Station Work?

Category: Guides & Safety Intent: Informational ~7 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

A portable power station is, at its core, three things in one box: a rechargeable battery, an inverter that turns that battery's DC power into the AC power your household outlets use, and a set of ports and a charge controller that manage everything coming in and going out. There's no engine, no fuel, and no combustion — which is the whole reason it can run indoors, silently, next to a bed or a tent.

The three parts, in plain terms

1. The battery

This is the "tank." It stores energy as chemical potential in a lithium battery pack, most commonly lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) in newer units, or lithium-ion (NMC) in older and some lighter-weight models. The size of the tank is measured in watt-hours (Wh) — see our full watt-hours explained guide for how to use that number. Bigger Wh means more total energy stored, not necessarily more power available at any one instant.

2. The inverter

Batteries store and release direct current (DC). Almost everything you plug into a wall outlet expects alternating current (AC). The inverter is the component that converts DC to AC so a power station can run normal household devices from its AC outlets. Inverter quality matters: a pure sine wave inverter produces power close to what the electrical grid delivers, which is safer for sensitive electronics; a modified sine wave inverter is cheaper to build but can cause some devices (particularly ones with motors, like certain medical equipment and refrigerator compressors) to run hot, run inefficiently, or refuse to run at all.

3. Ports, charge controller, and battery management system (BMS)

The ports are the physical connection points — AC outlets, USB-A, USB-C (often with fast-charging PD support), a car-style 12V port, and on larger units, a dedicated port for solar panel input. Behind the scenes, the battery management system constantly monitors cell temperature, voltage, and current to prevent overcharging, overheating, and the kind of imbalance that degrades a battery pack quickly. This is also why a power station can charge itself while simultaneously powering something else — a feature usually called pass-through charging.

Two numbers that describe every power station

SpecWhat it tells you
Watt-hours (Wh)Total stored energy — how long it can run something before it's empty.
Watts (W), continuous and surge/peakHow much power it can deliver at once — whether it can start and run a specific device.

Both numbers matter, and they answer different questions. A power station can have huge capacity (Wh) but a weak inverter (W) that still can't start a device with a high startup surge, like a refrigerator compressor or a sump pump motor. Conversely, a small power station might have plenty of instantaneous wattage but run out of stored energy (Wh) within an hour.

Worth knowing A device's startup ("surge") wattage is often 2–3× its running wattage, especially for anything with a motor or compressor. If a power station's spec sheet only lists one wattage number, that's usually the continuous rating — check separately whether it lists a surge rating before assuming it can start your appliance.

How charging actually works

Most power stations accept charge from more than one source, often simultaneously:

  • Wall (AC) charging — fastest for most units, plugged into a standard outlet.
  • Solar (DC) charging — via compatible solar panels; charging speed depends on panel wattage, sunlight quality, and the unit's maximum solar input rating, and is close to never as fast as wall charging.
  • Car charging — via a 12V car outlet, typically the slowest method, useful mainly for topping up on the road.

"Full charge time" specs on a product page usually refer to AC wall charging under ideal conditions — real-world solar charging times are meaningfully longer and vary with weather, so treat solar recharge estimates as a best case, not a guarantee.

How to Troubleshoot a Power Station That Won't Charge

Category: Guides Intent: Informational ~6 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

A power station that won't charge is rarely actually broken — it's usually one of a handful of specific, checkable causes. Work through these in order before assuming you need a warranty replacement.

Step 1: Rule out the simple stuff first

  1. Try a different outlet. A dead outlet, a tripped breaker, or a switched outlet in the "off" position is the single most common cause of "it won't charge" reports, and the easiest to rule out.
  2. Try a different cable, if you have one. Charging cables fail more often than the units themselves, especially ones that get coiled and uncoiled repeatedly.
  3. Check for a loose connection at both the wall end and the unit's charging port — debris or a slightly bent pin can prevent a full connection without being obviously visible.

Step 2: Check environmental conditions

Most lithium batteries — especially LiFePO4 — have a battery management system that will refuse to charge outside a safe temperature range, often somewhere around freezing on the low end. If the unit has been sitting in a cold car, garage, or outdoors, bring it to room temperature for an hour or two before trying again. This is a protective feature working as intended, not a fault.

Common misdiagnosis A unit that "won't charge" in cold weather but works fine once warmed up is not defective — this is expected lithium battery behavior. Don't return or disassemble a unit for this without first testing at room temperature.
Ad unit — in-article (responsive)

Step 3: Check for a battery management system lockout

If a battery has been deeply discharged — especially if it sat unused at very low charge for an extended period — some units' battery management systems enter a protective low-voltage lockout state that a standard charger can't recover from. Check your specific unit's manual for a "wake up" or reset procedure; many manufacturers publish one for exactly this scenario, sometimes involving a specific button sequence or a brief low-current trickle charge.

Step 4: Test with solar or car charging, if AC charging fails (or vice versa)

If one charging method fails but another works, the problem is likely isolated to that specific charging path (a damaged AC charging circuit, for instance) rather than the battery itself — useful information if you end up contacting warranty support, since it narrows down the likely fault.

Step 5: Check the display or app for error codes

Many mid-range and higher units show a specific error code or message when something is wrong, rather than just failing silently. Check your manual for what the code means before assuming the worst — some indicate simple, user-resolvable issues (like a port-specific fault) rather than a full unit failure.

When to contact warranty support

If you've ruled out the outlet, cable, connection, temperature, and lockout state, and tried multiple charging methods without success, it's reasonable to contact the manufacturer. Have ready: the model number, approximate age and usage history, what charging methods you've tried, and any error codes or display behavior — this speeds up the process considerably compared to just reporting "it doesn't charge."

Symptom-based quick reference

Display shows charging but percentage isn't movingCould be a slow trickle-charge state after deep discharge — see the lockout section above — or a display/sensor glitch; give it time before assuming a fault
Charges fine via wall outlet but not via car portOften a car port fuse (sometimes in the cable itself) or the vehicle's outlet not providing power with the engine off

When it's actually the wall power, not the unit

Before troubleshooting the power station itself further, confirm the outlet is actually delivering power: plug in a lamp or phone charger you know works. Outlets controlled by a wall switch (common in some rooms for a single outlet) are an easy thing to overlook if the switch happens to be off. If you're testing during an actual power outage and wondering why "charging" isn't working at all — that outlet has no power either, and you'll need an alternate charging method (car, solar, or a generator run safely outdoors) rather than continuing to troubleshoot the unit itself.

Portable Power Station Maintenance Checklist

Category: Guides Intent: Informational ~5 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

Portable power stations need very little maintenance compared to a gas generator — but the little they need matters, especially for a unit that might sit unused for months until an actual emergency.

Storage charge level

For long-term storage (weeks to months without use), most lithium batteries — LiFePO4 especially — hold up best stored at roughly 40-80% charge rather than fully charged or fully depleted. Storing at 100% for extended periods can accelerate capacity degradation; storing fully depleted risks the battery management system lockout covered in our troubleshooting guide. Check your specific manufacturer's recommendation, since exact guidance varies.

A practical maintenance schedule

IntervalTask
Every 1-3 monthsCheck charge level; top up or discharge to bring it back into the recommended storage range if it's drifted.
Every 3-6 monthsDo a full charge-and-use cycle — run it down partially with real devices rather than just checking the display, which also confirms it still performs as expected.
Before storm/travel seasonFull inspection and charge — see our hurricane season checklist for the full pre-season routine.
AnnuallyCheck for firmware updates (app-connected units), inspect cables and ports for wear, review the manufacturer's warranty terms and registration status.

Physical care

  • Keep ports clean and dry — dust and moisture in AC outlets or charging ports can cause connection issues over time; a soft brush or compressed air works better than liquid cleaners near electrical contacts.
  • Store at moderate temperature — avoid attics, uninsulated garages, or direct vehicle interior storage in climates with temperature extremes, both of which accelerate battery aging.
  • Inspect cables periodically for fraying, kinks, or exposed wire, especially ones that travel with camping or RV gear and get coiled/uncoiled often.
  • Keep the unit somewhat dust-free if it has cooling vents or a fan — accumulated dust can affect thermal performance under load.

App and firmware maintenance (for connected units)

If your unit has app connectivity, check periodically for firmware updates — manufacturers sometimes release updates that improve charging behavior, fix reported bugs, or adjust battery management thresholds. Skipping updates for years isn't necessarily harmful, but checking in occasionally, especially after a manufacturer support interaction, is good practice.

A pre-emergency check, not just routine maintenance

The worst time to discover a maintenance issue is during an actual outage. Build a habit of testing your unit under real load — not just checking the charge percentage on the display — at least twice a year, ideally timed ahead of your region's known higher-risk seasons.

Climate-specific storage considerations

  • Hot climates: avoid storage in attics, vehicle interiors, or anywhere that regularly exceeds roughly 85-95°F (check your manufacturer's specific upper limit) — sustained heat is one of the most significant accelerators of battery capacity loss over time. A closet or interior storage room is generally far better than a garage in a hot climate.
  • Cold climates: extreme cold storage (well below freezing for extended periods) can also degrade battery health, separate from the temporary charging restriction covered in our troubleshooting guide. Bring the unit into conditioned space for storage where practical, rather than an unheated shed or garage through a full winter.
  • Humid climates: ports and connectors are more prone to corrosion in consistently humid conditions — periodic inspection matters more, and a silica gel packet in a storage case can help in genuinely damp storage environments.

What actually voids warranties

Warranty terms vary by manufacturer, but common exclusions worth knowing before an issue comes up include: physical damage from drops or water intrusion beyond the unit's rated ingress protection, use of third-party chargers or cables not approved by the manufacturer, unauthorized disassembly or repair attempts, and use outside the stated operating temperature range. Register your unit with the manufacturer if that's offered — it's often required to streamline a warranty claim, and it's a simple step that's easy to skip and later regret when you actually need it.

Frequently asked questions

Is it bad to leave a power station on the charger all the time?

Most modern units have charge-management circuitry that prevents overcharging damage from being left plugged in, but for long-term storage, actively managing the charge level to the 40-80% range is still generally better than leaving it at 100% indefinitely — check your manufacturer's specific guidance.

How often should I actually use my power station if it's just for emergencies?

A real-load test every 3-6 months is a reasonable baseline — enough to catch problems before an emergency, without excessive wear from frequent full cycles.

Pure Sine Wave vs Modified Sine Wave: Why It Matters

Category: Guides Intent: Informational ~4 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

This spec gets buried in the fine print, but it determines whether some of your devices run cleanly, run poorly, or don't run at all.

What the terms actually mean

Household electrical grid power follows a smooth, continuous wave pattern — a pure sine wave. A power station's inverter converts stored DC battery power into AC power for its outlets, and how closely that conversion mimics the grid's smooth wave determines the type:

  • Pure sine wave — closely replicates grid power. Compatible with essentially everything, including sensitive electronics.
  • Modified sine wave — a cruder, stepped approximation. Cheaper to produce, but not compatible with everything.

What runs poorly (or not at all) on modified sine wave

Device typeTypical issue on modified sine wave

Devices with AC motors (some refrigerators, power tools)Can run hotter and less efficiently, potentially shortening motor life
Audio/video equipmentAudible humming or buzzing is a common complaint
Laptop chargers, some electronicsOften fine, but not universally — some run hot or inefficiently
Simple resistive loads (basic lights, some heaters)Generally unaffected
If you have medical equipment Don't assume — check directly with your device's manufacturer or your DME provider about pure vs. modified sine wave compatibility before relying on any power station for medical equipment. This is exactly the kind of detail worth confirming before an emergency, not during one.
Ad unit — in-article (responsive)

The current state of the market

Most mid-range and higher portable power stations sold today use pure sine wave inverters — it's become closer to standard than the exception as costs have come down. Modified sine wave units still exist, typically at the lower end of the price range. The spec is usually listed directly on the product page or in the manual; if it isn't stated anywhere, that's worth treating as a red flag rather than assuming it's pure sine wave by default.

Bottom line

Unless you're certain every device you'll ever run is a simple resistive load (basic lighting, for instance) and price is the only priority, pure sine wave is worth paying for. The cost difference has narrowed considerably, and the downside of guessing wrong — a medical device that won't run, or electronics that degrade over time — outweighs the modest savings.

Watt-Hours Explained: How to Calculate the Power Station Size You Need

Category: Guides Intent: Informational ~6 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

Watt-hours (Wh) is the single most important number on a power station's spec sheet, and it's the one most often skimmed past. It tells you how much total energy the unit stores — the size of the tank, not the size of the tap. Get comfortable with this one calculation and every sizing question gets much easier.

The core formula

Energy used (in watt-hours) equals the power draw of a device (in watts) multiplied by how long you run it (in hours):

Formula Watt-hours needed = Watts × Hours

Run a 60-watt device for 5 hours and you've used 300 Wh. Run a 10-watt device for 30 hours and you've also used 300 Wh. Same energy, very different power draw and duration — which is exactly why a single "watts" number on a box doesn't tell you what you need to know on its own.

Finding a device's actual wattage

Don't guess — check the device itself. Most appliances and electronics list their power draw on a label (often near the plug or on the underside), usually as watts (W) directly, or as volts × amps, which you can multiply together to get watts. If a label only lists amps (A) for a standard US 120V device, multiply amps by 120 to estimate watts.

Typical deviceRunning watts (approximate — check your label)
Wi-Fi router / modem5–15W
Laptop45–65W
LED light bulb8–12W each
CPAP machine (no heated humidifier)30–60W
Refrigerator (running, not starting)100–200W
Sump pump (running)800–1,300W

These are general ranges to sanity-check your math against — not a substitute for the number printed on your specific appliance, which can vary by model and age.

Why "usable" watt-hours is smaller than "rated" watt-hours

A power station rated at 1,000Wh will not actually deliver 1,000Wh of usable output. Between inverter efficiency losses, battery chemistry limits, and the battery management system reserving a small buffer, real-world usable capacity typically runs around 80–90% of the rated figure. When sizing, it's safer to plan against roughly 85% of the rated Wh, not the number printed on the box.

Ad unit — in-article (responsive)

Worked example

Say you want to run a Wi-Fi router (10W) and four LED lamps (10W each, 40W total) for a 10-hour overnight outage:

  • Total running watts: 10 + 40 = 50W
  • Energy needed: 50W × 10 hours = 500Wh
  • Accounting for ~85% usable capacity: 500 ÷ 0.85 ≈ 590Wh

That points you toward a power station rated at roughly 600–700Wh or larger for this specific load — with some margin, since outages rarely end exactly on schedule. For a full walk-through that also checks whether the unit can actually start your devices (not just run them), see What Size Power Station Do I Need for My Home?, which includes an interactive calculator.

Frequently asked questions

Is watt-hours the same as amp-hours?

No. Amp-hours (Ah) measures charge at a specific voltage, while watt-hours (Wh) measures total energy and already accounts for voltage — which is why Wh is the more useful number when comparing units that may run at different internal voltages. To convert, multiply amp-hours by voltage.

Does cold weather reduce a power station's watt-hour capacity?

Yes. Lithium batteries generally deliver less usable capacity in cold conditions, and charging near freezing can be slowed or restricted by the battery management system. Add extra margin beyond the standard 85% usable-capacity estimate for cold-weather use.

If I have two power stations, do their watt-hours add together?

For total available energy, yes — two 500Wh units give roughly 1,000Wh combined. In practice you'll typically switch appliances between them manually rather than combining their output, unless the specific units support linking.

Why do two power stations with the same watt-hour rating perform differently?

Watt-hours describes stored energy, not conversion efficiency. Inverter efficiency, battery chemistry, and battery management system behavior all affect how much of the rated Wh actually reaches your devices.

How Long Will a Portable Power Station Run My Refrigerator?

Category: Home Backup Intent: Informational ~5 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

A refrigerator is one of the trickier appliances to size for — not because it draws much power while running, but because of the surge it demands every time the compressor kicks on. Here's how to work out both numbers.

Running watts vs. starting watts

Once a refrigerator's compressor is running, most standard residential units draw somewhere in the range of 100–200 running watts, depending on size, age, and efficiency rating. But starting the compressor from a stop can briefly demand 2–3× that figure for a fraction of a second to a couple of seconds. Check your unit's specific rating — often printed on a sticker inside the fridge or on the back panel, or in the owner's manual — rather than assuming a number.

Why this trips people up A power station can show plenty of remaining watt-hours and still fail to start a refrigerator if its surge/peak wattage rating is lower than the fridge's startup demand. Capacity (Wh) and power delivery (W) are two separate specs — you need both to check out.

Once it's running, use the watt-hours formula from our watt-hours explained guide: divide the power station's usable watt-hours by the fridge's running wattage.

Hurricane Season Power Outage Prep Checklist (2026)

Category: Home Backup Intent: Informational ~5 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

Hurricane-related outages are rarely a surprise — there's usually days of forecast warning. The households that come through comfortably are the ones who prep before the storm is named, not after the shelves are empty.

4–6 weeks before hurricane season

  • Fully charge your power station and check it actually holds charge — battery capacity fades over time, and the start of the season is the time to find that out, not mid-storm.
  • Confirm your power station's wattage rating still covers what you plan to run (see our sizing calculator if your household's needs have changed).
  • If you also have solar panels for charging, check cables and connectors for wear.

When a storm is in the forecast (48–72 hours out)

  • Charge every power station and battery bank to 100%.
  • Charge phones, laptops, and any medical device batteries fully.
  • Fill and freeze water bottles or ice packs — they extend refrigerator/cooler runtime and double as backup ice.
  • Confirm you have manual charging cables (not just wireless pads) — they're more power-efficient and more reliable if things get chaotic.
  • If you rely on medical equipment, review your specific plan — see our CPAP power backup guide if that applies to your household.
Don't forget Set your refrigerator and freezer to their coldest safe settings a day before impact — a colder start buys extra hours before food safety becomes a concern if the power does go out.

During the outage

  • Prioritize your must-run list (see sizing guide) — don't run everything at once if capacity is limited.
  • Keep the power station somewhere dry, ventilated, and out of direct flood risk — not directly on the floor in a flood-prone area.
  • If you also have a gas generator, run it outdoors only — see power station vs. gas generator for the safety details.
  • Recharge via car or solar during any daylight/driving windows, since wall power obviously won't be available.

After the storm

  • Let a fully depleted power station cool before recharging if it's been under heavy load.
  • Recharge fully and store it somewhere temperature-stable ahead of the next storm in the season — most lithium batteries hold up best stored around 40–80% charge if it'll sit unused for a while, so top back up closer to the next expected use rather than leaving it at 100% for months.

Portable Power Station vs Gas Generator: Pros, Cons, and Costs

Category: Home Backup Intent: Comparative ~6 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

Both keep the power on during an outage, but they're better suited to different situations. Here's an honest comparison — not a case for one being categorically better than the other.

When a power station makes more sense

  • You live somewhere gas generators aren't practical or allowed (apartments, condos, some HOAs)
  • Your priority is medical equipment, communication, and a fridge — not the whole house
  • You want something you can also use for camping, RV trips, or as a everyday backup for outages measured in hours, not days
  • Noise and fumes near your home are a real concern (attached housing, close neighbors)

  • You expect multi-day outages and need to run larger appliances, well pumps, or HVAC
  • You have safe, ventilated space to run it (never indoors, a garage, or near windows/doors)

What Size Power Station Do I Need for My Home?

Category: Home Backup Intent: Informational ~6 min read + calculator

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

Sizing a power station for home backup comes down to answering two separate questions: what do you need to keep running, and for how long? Skip either one and you'll either overspend on capacity you don't need, or come up short halfway through an outage.

Step 1: Decide what actually needs to stay on

Most homes don't need — or can't realistically run — everything during an outage. A useful exercise is sorting your devices into three tiers:

  • Tier 1 — must stay on: medical equipment, a sump pump, a refrigerator with food in it, communication (router, phones charged).
  • Tier 2 — nice to have: a few lights, a laptop, a fan.
  • Tier 3 — skip it: anything heat-producing (space heaters, hair dryers, toasters) or a whole HVAC system — these draw far more power than a portable unit is realistically sized to handle.

Step 3: Round up, then round up again

Real outages don't end on schedule, and cold weather reduces usable battery capacity. It's reasonable to size for at least 25–30% more capacity than your calculated minimum, especially if medical equipment or food safety is involved.

When a portable power station isn't enough

If your list of "must stay on" items includes HVAC, well pump, or multiple major appliances, or the expected outage could run several days, a portable power station is likely the wrong tool on its own — see Portable Power Station vs Gas Generator for where that line typically falls, and consider a professionally installed whole-home backup system instead.

Frequently asked questions

Can one power station run multiple appliances at once?

Yes, as long as their combined running wattage stays under the unit's continuous wattage rating and your outlets/ports can accommodate them — the calculator above adds multiple appliances together for exactly this reason.

What happens if I plug in more than the power station can handle?

Most units trip an internal safety cutoff and shut off output rather than being damaged, though this varies by manufacturer. Check your specific unit's overload behavior in its manual rather than testing it during an actual outage.

Should I size for my average load or my peak load?

Size the wattage rating for your peak simultaneous load, including startup surges, and size the watt-hour capacity for your total energy use over the full outage. They're two different numbers answering two different questions — both covered above.

Is it worth buying a bigger power station than I calculate I need?

Generally yes, within reason. Outages rarely end exactly on schedule, cold weather reduces usable capacity, and margin costs less upfront than running out of power costs in inconvenience — see our worked scenarios for how much margin similar situations typically call for.

How to Build a Medical Emergency Power Backup Plan

Category: Medical & Accessibility Intent: Informational ~5 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

A portable power station is one layer of a medical emergency power plan — not the whole plan. This is about building the layers around it, so a single point of failure doesn't become a genuine emergency.

Start with your care team Your durable medical equipment (DME) supplier, home health agency, or physician can confirm your device's exact power needs and may have specific backup guidance or approved equipment for your situation. This guide is a general framework, not a substitute for that conversation.

Layer 1: Know your equipment's real power draw

Get the actual wattage for every piece of equipment you depend on — from the label, the manual, or your DME provider — not an estimate. See Watt-Hours Explained for how to read and use that number.

Layer 2: Register for utility medical priority status, if available

Many utilities offer a "medical baseline" or "critical care" program that can provide advance outage notice, priority restoration, or other support for customers with medical equipment needs. Contact your utility directly to ask what's available in your area — this is a step a battery alone can't replace.

Layer 3: Size backup power conservatively

Plan for meaningfully more than one night's or one outage's worth of capacity — outages don't reliably end on schedule. See our CPAP-specific guide for one worked example of this approach; the same conservative-margin principle applies to other equipment.

Ad unit — in-article (responsive)

Layer 4: Have a backup to the backup

  • Know the nearest location with reliable power you could relocate to if backup power fails or runs out — a family member's home, a hospital, or a designated community resource.
  • Keep your DME provider's and physician's contact information somewhere accessible without power (written down, not just on a phone that needs charging).
  • If you use battery-dependent mobility equipment too, factor its charging needs into the same plan rather than treating it separately.

Layer 5: Test the whole plan before you need it

Run your actual equipment on your actual backup power source for a realistic stretch of time, under normal conditions — not for the first time during an actual outage. This is the step most plans skip, and it's the one most likely to surface a problem while there's still time to fix it.

Portable Power for Wheelchair and Mobility Scooter Charging

Category: Medical & Accessibility Intent: Informational ~6 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

Mobility device batteries are meaningfully different from the power station's own battery — the charging profile, the stakes of getting it wrong, and the backup planning all need separate consideration.

Start with your equipment provider Your mobility equipment supplier or manufacturer can confirm your specific device's charger type, wattage, and any charging restrictions. This guide is a general framework, not a substitute for that conversation — especially before relying on any backup power source for equipment you depend on for mobility.

Battery types commonly used in wheelchairs and scooters

Battery typeCommon inCharging consideration
Sealed lead-acid (SLA/AGM)Many power wheelchairs and mobility scootersHeavier, typically charged via the device's own charger unit rather than directly from a power station's DC ports
Lithium-ionIncreasingly common in newer, lighter mobility devicesDifferent charging profile than lead-acid; check charger compatibility carefully

In almost all cases, you'll charge the mobility device using its own dedicated charger, plugged into the power station's AC outlet — not by connecting the device's battery directly to the power station. This makes the power station's AC output (and inverter type — see pure vs modified sine wave) the relevant spec, not its DC ports.

Sizing for a mobility device charger

Most mobility device chargers draw somewhere in the range of 50-150W while charging — check your specific charger's label for its actual rating. Using the method in Watt-Hours Explained, a full charge cycle (which can take several hours depending on the device and how depleted the battery is) might use somewhere in the range of 300-900Wh — plan capacity around your device's actual charger rating and typical charge time, not a generic estimate.

Ad unit — in-article (responsive)

Travel considerations

If you travel with both a mobility device and a portable power station, be aware that airlines have specific, and different, rules for lithium batteries (including those in power stations and lithium-powered mobility devices) versus sealed lead-acid batteries. Airlines and the TSA publish specific watt-hour thresholds and documentation requirements — check directly with your airline well before travel, since rules and enforcement can vary and change.

Building redundancy, not just capacity

  • Keep the mobility device itself charged proactively rather than running it down and relying on backup power to recover it during an emergency.
  • Know your utility's medical/mobility equipment priority programs, if available — similar to programs described in our medical emergency power planning guide.
  • Have a plan beyond the battery — knowing where you could get emergency charging access (a neighbor, a community location) matters as much as owning backup power equipment.

Understanding your device's charge cycle

Mobility device batteries are typically designed around daily or near-daily charging rather than deep, infrequent cycles — many manufacturers recommend charging overnight after each day of significant use rather than waiting until the battery is nearly empty. This matters for backup power planning: your realistic need during an outage is often a full or near-full overnight charge cycle (potentially several hours, given these chargers are frequently lower-wattage than a laptop charger), not a quick top-up. Check your specific device's manual for its recommended charge routine and typical full-charge duration, since this varies meaningfully between device models and battery types.

Building a travel charging kit

If you travel regularly with a mobility device, a dedicated small kit reduces risk beyond just packing the charger:

  • The device's original charger, plus verification that your destination's outlet voltage/plug type is compatible if traveling internationally — mobility device chargers aren't always universal-voltage the way some consumer electronics chargers are.
  • Documentation of your battery type and watt-hour rating, since airlines may ask for this at check-in, particularly for lithium batteries above certain thresholds.
  • A portable power station sized to at least one full charge cycle, tested at home with your actual device before relying on it while traveling.
  • Contact information for a local mobility equipment supplier at your destination, if the trip is long enough that equipment issues becoming a real risk feels worth planning for.

Best Solar Setup for Boondocking in an RV

Category: RV & Van Life Intent: Commercial ~7 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

Boondocking (camping without hookups) lives or dies on your energy budget. Here's how the pieces of a boondocking solar setup actually fit together, and where most setups fall short of expectations.

The three components of a boondocking solar setup

1. Solar panels

Two broad types serve RV boondocking: roof-mounted rigid panels (permanently installed, charge whenever parked in sun, no setup required) and portable/foldable panels (set up on the ground, can be angled toward the sun and moved to follow shade, but require manual setup and are vulnerable to being left behind or damaged). Many boondockers eventually run both — roof panels for passive daily charging, portable panels for topping up when parked in partial shade.

2. Charge controller: PWM vs MPPT

PWMMPPT
CostLowerHigher
EfficiencyLower — panel voltage is effectively wasted down to match battery voltageHigher — converts excess panel voltage into additional current, extracting more usable power
Best suited forSmall, simple setups where the panel and battery voltages are already closely matchedLarger setups, cold climates (higher panel voltage), or when panel and battery voltage differ significantly

For most boondocking setups beyond a single small panel, MPPT's efficiency gain is usually worth the added cost over the life of the system.

3. Battery bank

Whether that's your RV's house battery or a separate portable power station, this is what stores what the panels collect for use after dark. See Portable Power Station vs RV House Battery if you're deciding between upgrading the existing bank or adding a separate portable unit.

Building a realistic daily energy budget

Boondocking budgets typically look leaner than home backup budgets — lighting, device charging, a 12V fridge, water pump, and maybe a laptop, rather than a full-size refrigerator and multiple rooms of lighting.

LoadWattsHours/dayDaily Wh
12V fridge50W8h effective400

Water pump50W0.5h25

Daily total~625 Wh/day

Matching solar input to this budget realistically (see how many solar panels you actually need) means a 200-300W panel setup can often keep pace with this kind of light daily use in good conditions — though multi-day cloud cover will still draw down your battery bank faster than panels replenish it.

Where boondocking solar setups commonly fall short

  • Underestimating shade and parking angle. Tree cover, canyon walls, or simply parking with panels facing the wrong direction can cut real output well below what a sunny-day spec sheet promises.
  • Skipping the charge controller upgrade. A PWM controller paired with a larger panel array wastes a meaningful fraction of your investment.
  • No plan for extended cloudy stretches. A generator or a plan to move to a charging location is worth having even in a well-designed solar setup, since multi-day low-sun periods happen.

Seasonal and geographic variability

The same panel setup performs very differently across seasons and latitude. Shorter winter days mean fewer total sun-hours even where skies are clear; lower winter sun angles reduce output further unless panels are angled to compensate (easier with portable panels you can tilt than with flat roof-mounted arrays). Boondocking further north or in a winter season should assume meaningfully less daily solar recovery than the same setup would deliver in summer at a lower latitude — plan battery reserve accordingly rather than assuming your summer numbers hold year-round.

Common wiring mistakes that quietly reduce output

  • Undersized wire gauge between panels and controller — voltage drop over a run of wire that's too thin for the distance and current wastes real power before it ever reaches the battery.
  • Poor or corroded connections — a loose or corroded connector can meaningfully cut output without being obviously visible; check connections periodically, especially after rough roads or long trips.
  • Panels wired in a configuration mismatched to the charge controller — series vs. parallel wiring changes the voltage and current the controller sees, and getting this wrong can mean the system underperforms even with correctly rated components.
  • No fusing or overcurrent protection — beyond a performance issue, this is a real safety gap; solar wiring should be fused appropriately regardless of system size.

If you're not confident designing this yourself, a one-time consultation with an RV solar installer to check or design the wiring plan is often worth the cost relative to a system that silently underperforms for years.

Is MPPT always worth the extra cost over PWM?

For anything beyond a small single-panel setup, generally yes — the efficiency gain compounds over the life of the system. For a very small, simple setup with closely matched panel and battery voltage, PWM can be an acceptable cost-saving choice.

There's no universal answer — it depends on your battery capacity relative to daily use and your tolerance for reduced power use during low-sun stretches. Many boondockers plan for 2-3 days of battery reserve beyond daily solar input as a reasonable buffer.

How to Size a Power Station for Your RV Air Conditioner

Category: RV & Van Life Intent: Informational ~7 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

RV air conditioners are the single biggest power draw most rigs have — and the appliance most likely to make a portable power station look badly undersized on paper, even when it isn't quite as hopeless as the numbers first suggest.

Why RV AC units draw so much

RV air conditioners are rated in BTUs (British Thermal Units), commonly 13,500 or 15,000 BTU for standard rooftop units. Converting BTU to watts isn't direct, but as a rough planning figure, a 13,500 BTU unit typically draws somewhere in the range of 1,200-1,600 running watts — and considerably more on startup, since the compressor has to overcome standing pressure in the refrigerant lines every time it kicks on.

Unit sizeApproximate running wattsApproximate startup surge
13,500 BTU1,200-1,600W2,800-3,500W+ (without soft start)
15,000 BTU1,500-1,800W3,300-4,000W+ (without soft start)

General planning ranges — check your specific unit's nameplate rating, which is the only fully reliable source.

The single most important upgrade: soft-start technology

A soft-start device (sometimes called a "soft start capacitor" or sold as an add-on kit) ramps the compressor up gradually instead of hitting it with full startup current all at once. This can cut startup surge by roughly half or more, which is frequently the difference between a power station's inverter being able to start the AC unit at all versus tripping an overload every time. If you're serious about running RV AC from a portable power station, a soft-start device is usually a better first investment than a bigger battery.

Why this matters more than watt-hours here Running out of stored energy (Wh) partway through is a capacity problem you can plan around. Failing to start the compressor at all (W) is a hard stop regardless of how much energy is stored — check the surge rating match before anything else.

Working out realistic runtime

Using the method from Watt-Hours Explained: a 1,400W running load for 3 hours needs 4,200Wh raw, or roughly 4,950Wh adjusted for usable capacity. That's a genuinely large power station — often at or beyond what a single portable unit provides — which is why most owners either accept limited AC runtime (an hour or two to cool down before switching off) or pair a large expandable-capacity unit with solar/generator recharging rather than expecting all-day AC from stored capacity alone.

A more realistic use pattern

  • Cool down before bed, then switch to fans — run AC for 1-2 hours to bring the interior temperature down, then rely on 12V fans (much lower draw) to maintain comfort overnight.
  • Pre-cool while on shore power or generator, then use the power station only for the stretch without either.
  • Consider whether the trip genuinely needs AC from a portable unit versus planning around campgrounds with hookups for the hottest stretches.

How this compares to the RV's existing systems

If your RV already has a substantial house battery bank and inverter capable of handling this load, a portable power station may be solving a problem you don't have — see Portable Power Station vs RV House Battery for that comparison before buying a second, separate unit just for AC.

Alternative cooling strategies worth pairing with (or instead of) AC

  • 12V roof and box fans. Draw a small fraction of what AC does (often 20-50W) and can meaningfully improve comfort through air circulation alone, especially combined with shade and ventilation.
  • Reflective window coverings and awnings. Reducing solar heat gain in the first place lowers how much cooling you need at all — genuinely effective and draws zero power.
  • Parking strategy. Orienting the rig to keep the most-used side shaded, and moving with shade through the day where possible, reduces interior heat load before you even turn on a fan.
  • Insulation and sealing. Older or poorly sealed RVs lose the cooling effort you do generate faster — weatherstripping and vent insulation are unglamorous but effective for the power you do spend.

Combining these with the "pre-cool then fans" pattern described above often gets closer to comfortable than expecting a portable power station to run AC continuously ever will.

How to measure your specific AC unit's actual draw

Rather than relying on the general BTU-based estimates above, you can measure your exact unit's real running and startup wattage with an inline watt meter (relatively inexpensive) or a clamp meter on the circuit, if you're comfortable working with your RV's electrical panel — or ask an RV electrician to do this as part of a broader electrical assessment. Knowing your unit's real numbers, rather than a BTU-based estimate, lets you size a soft-start device and any power station purchase to your actual equipment instead of a general range that may run higher or lower than your specific AC unit.

Frequently asked questions

Can a portable power station run an RV air conditioner all night?

Usually not from stored capacity alone without a very large or expandable-capacity unit — the energy math typically runs into the thousands of watt-hours for even a few hours of runtime. A pre-cool-then-fans strategy is more realistic for most portable setups.

Do I need a soft-start device if my power station has a high peak wattage rating?

It depends on the specific match between your AC unit's startup surge and the power station's peak rating — a soft-start device lowers the AC's demand rather than raising the power station's supply, so it's often the more cost-effective fix even with a capable unit.

How Many Solar Panels Do You Need to Charge a Power Station?

Category: Solar & Off-Grid Intent: Informational ~5 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

Solar panel wattage gets marketed as a straightforward number, but real-world charging speed depends on more than what's printed on the panel — and it's rarely as fast as the "full charge time" spec suggests.

The basic math

A power station's solar input is limited by two things: the panel's rated wattage, and the unit's maximum solar input rating (check the spec sheet — many models cap solar input well below what you might assume). In ideal, direct, cloudless midday sun, a panel produces close to its rated wattage. Realistically, expect meaningfully less — often in the range of 60-80% of rated output — once you account for panel angle, partial cloud cover, temperature, and time of day.

Worked example A 200W-rated solar panel, in good but non-ideal conditions, might realistically deliver something like 120-160W. Charging a 1,000Wh power station from empty under those conditions takes roughly 6-8+ hours of good daylight — not the 5 hours a naive 1000÷200 calculation would suggest.

What actually affects real-world charging speed

  • Panel angle relative to the sun — a flat panel misses significant output compared to one angled directly at the sun.
  • Cloud cover — even light cloud can cut output substantially; heavy cloud can drop it to a small fraction of rated wattage.
  • Panel temperature — solar panels lose some efficiency as they heat up, which matters on hot, sunny days.
  • The power station's maximum solar input rating — pairing a 400W panel with a unit that only accepts 200W of solar input wastes the panel's extra capacity.
  • Season and latitude — shorter winter days and lower sun angles reduce both available hours and per-hour output.

A more realistic planning approach

Rather than relying on a manufacturer's best-case "full charge in X hours" claim, plan around roughly half that panel's rated wattage as your realistic average hourly input across a full day of mixed conditions, and treat solar as a way to maintain charge over multiple days rather than a fast, on-demand recharge method. For any situation where you're relying on power overnight — camping, medical equipment, an extended outage — don't plan around same-day solar recovery working perfectly.

How to Build a Balcony Solar System for Apartment Living

Category: Solar & Off-Grid Intent: Informational ~6 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

Balcony solar is genuinely achievable for apartment living, but the practical obstacles are usually legal and physical, not electrical — worth working through in that order.

Check your lease and HOA rules before buying anything

This is the step most guides skip, and it's the one most likely to actually block you. Many leases and HOA agreements restrict exterior modifications, including anything visibly mounted to a balcony railing or attached to the building structure. Before purchasing equipment:

  • Read your lease's clauses on "alterations," "exterior modifications," and "attachments" specifically — solar equipment often isn't named explicitly, which cuts both ways.
  • Ask your landlord or HOA directly, in writing, rather than assuming a gray area works in your favor.
  • Check local building and fire code for balcony weight limits and any restrictions on obstructing exits — even where the lease is silent, code may not be.

Two approaches, in order of complexity

1. Portable panel + power station (simplest, most portable-friendly)

A foldable or rigid portable solar panel angled on the balcony floor or leaned against the railing, feeding a portable power station indoors. No permanent mounting, no electrical work, and it moves with you. This is the approach most apartment renters should start with, and it pairs directly with the sizing method in Watt-Hours Explained.

2. Balcony-mount microinverter systems (more capable, more regulatory complexity)

Small grid-tied panel systems that plug into a standard outlet and feed power back into your apartment's circuit are sold in some markets, but their legality and utility acceptance varies significantly by country, state, and even individual utility — some explicitly prohibit plug-in grid-tied solar without a licensed electrician's installation and utility approval, regardless of the product's own safety certifications. If you're considering this route, verify with your specific utility and local electrical code before purchasing, not after.

Physical and safety considerations

  • Wind load. Panels leaned or mounted on an upper-floor balcony can act like a sail in strong wind — secure them properly, and consider taking portable panels inside during storms.
  • Weight limits. Most residential balconies handle normal furniture loads fine, but check before adding a heavier fixed array, especially on older buildings.
  • Cable routing. Running a cable from an outdoor panel to an indoor power station means a door or window will have a cable through it — a flat, low-profile cable pass-through reduces the draft and pinch-point issues of just closing a window on a round cable.
  • Fire egress. Never block a balcony's function as a fire escape route with panels or equipment, even temporarily.

Realistic expectations for output

A single balcony-sized portable panel (typically 100-200W) in a good sun-exposed location can meaningfully offset device charging and lighting, but won't replace grid power for an apartment's larger loads. Treat it as a genuine supplement — and a resilience layer for outages — rather than a path to going off-grid in an apartment setting. Foldable/portable panelRigid panel leaned or clamped in place

For most apartment balconies, a foldable panel's flexibility and easy storage outweighs the fixed panel's simplicity — you can set it up only when you're actually charging and remove it the rest of the time, which also sidesteps some of the "permanent exterior modification" lease concerns discussed above.

Maximizing output from a small, space-constrained setup

  • Chase the sun through the day if you're home — re-angling a portable panel every hour or two captures meaningfully more energy than leaving it at a fixed morning angle all day.
  • Prioritize an unobstructed few hours over a longer but shaded exposure — a panel in full sun for 3 clear hours often outperforms one in dappled, partial shade for 8.
  • Keep panel surfaces clean — dust and pollen buildup measurably reduces output over weeks, especially in a fixed outdoor location.
  • Pair with a power station sized to your actual space constraint, not an oversized unit that never gets recharged in one sunny session — see Watt-Hours Explained for matching capacity to realistic daily solar input.

LiFePO4 vs Lithium-Ion Power Stations: Which Battery Lasts Longer?

Category: Solar & Off-Grid Intent: Comparative ~5 min read

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

Most current portable power stations use one of two lithium battery chemistries — LiFePO4 (lithium iron phosphate) or NMC/NCA lithium-ion — and the difference between them matters more than most spec sheets make obvious.

The core trade-off

LiFePO4Lithium-ion (NMC/NCA)
Typical cycle lifeOften rated around 2,000-4,000+ full charge cycles to 80% capacityOften rated around 500-1,000 full charge cycles to 80% capacity
Energy densityLower — heavier for the same capacityHigher — lighter for the same capacity
Thermal stabilityGenerally more stable, lower fire risk under stressGenerally less thermally stable than LiFePO4
Typical use case todayHome backup, RV, and other units where cycle life and safety margin matter more than weightUltralight and budget-oriented units where weight or price is the priority

Exact cycle-life and safety figures vary by manufacturer and specific cell — check the individual product's documentation rather than assuming these ranges apply universally.

Why cycle life matters more than it sounds like it should

"Cycle life" is how many full charge-discharge cycles a battery can handle before it degrades to roughly 80% of its original capacity. For a power station charged and drained occasionally for outages, this might not matter much either chemistry lasts years. But for daily use, frequent camping trips, or off-grid living where the battery cycles often, the gap between roughly 500 cycles and roughly 3,000+ cycles is the difference between replacing (or noticeably degrading) a unit within a couple of years versus getting a decade or more of regular use out of it.

Ad unit — in-article (responsive)

Which one should influence your decision

  • Home backup, used occasionally: either chemistry is reasonable; LiFePO4's longer shelf life sitting at partial charge is a modest advantage for equipment that mostly sits waiting.
  • Frequent camping, van life, or daily cycling: LiFePO4's cycle-life advantage compounds significantly over years of regular use.
  • Backpacking or anywhere weight is the dominant constraint: lithium-ion's better energy density may be worth the shorter cycle life trade-off.
  • Anywhere the unit will be indoors near people overnight (bedroom, tent) — LiFePO4's thermal stability profile is generally considered the more conservative choice, though both chemistries are used safely in consumer products when properly engineered.

How to check which chemistry a specific unit uses

Battery chemistry is usually listed directly in the product specifications — look for "LiFePO4," "LFP," "NMC," or "NCA." If it isn't listed anywhere on the product page, that's worth asking the manufacturer directly before buying, since it materially affects how the unit will perform for you over time.

By the WattHour Editorial Team · Verified against manufacturer documentation and established electrical fundamentals · Last updated September 2026

Off-grid cabin power needs span a huge range — from a weekend retreat that just needs lights and phone charging, to full-time living with everything a house normally runs. The right power setup depends entirely on where on that spectrum your cabin actually sits.

Defining the spectrum

What a permanent system adds that a portable unit doesn't

  • Properly sized battery banks — often lithium or well-designed lead-acid banks sized in multiple kWh, far beyond what portable units offer.
  • A dedicated inverter/charger sized for whole-cabin loads, rather than a single unit's port limitations.
  • Integration with a generator as an automatic backup, rather than manual switching.
  • Proper electrical code compliance for permanent wiring — relevant for insurance, resale, and safety in a way a portable setup isn't.

The trade-off is cost and complexity: a permanent system is a real electrical project, typically requiring professional design and installation, versus a portable setup you can buy and start using the same day.

Where portable power stations remain the better choice

  • The cabin sees genuinely occasional use, and you don't want to invest in a permanent installation for something used a few weekends a year.
  • You're not ready to commit to the cabin's long-term use pattern yet and want flexibility to change your setup later.
  • Budget favors starting small and expanding — a portable unit with expandable battery capacity can grow with you without a full system redesign.
  • You need the power source to also serve other uses (camping, home backup) rather than staying permanently at the cabin.

A hybrid path many cabin owners take

Start with a portable power station and portable panels for the first season, track actual usage patterns (which is more reliable than estimating upfront), then decide whether a permanent system is justified based on real data rather than a guess. This avoids over-investing in a permanent system sized for a usage pattern that turns out to be wrong.
